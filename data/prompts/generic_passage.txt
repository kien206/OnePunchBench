Rivers have shaped the growth of towns and cities for thousands of years. Before roads were paved and railways were laid, water was the easiest way to move heavy goods over long distances. A barge loaded with grain, timber or stone could travel downstream with little effort, and settlements grew wherever boats needed to stop, unload or wait for better weather. Many of the oldest cities in the world sit on the banks of a large river, close to a ford, a bridge or a natural harbor.

Water also made farming possible. Seasonal floods spread fine silt across the surrounding plains, renewing the soil each year. Farmers learned to predict the floods, to store water in basins and to guide it through ditches and canals. Over time, these irrigation systems became large public works that required planning, labor and record keeping. Some historians argue that the need to manage water helped early states develop written laws, calendars and systems of measurement.

Rivers brought dangers as well. A flood that arrived too early or rose too high could destroy a harvest and wash away houses. Standing water encouraged the spread of disease, and a river that changed its course could leave a busy port stranded far from the shore. Communities responded by building levees, draining marshes and moving to higher ground, but the balance between benefit and risk was never fully settled.

Today most freight travels by road, rail or sea, yet rivers remain important. They supply drinking water and cooling water for power plants, carry wastewater away from cities and support fisheries and wildlife. Dams store water for dry seasons and generate electricity, although they also trap sediment, block the movement of fish and change the landscape downstream. Engineers and ecologists now try to manage rivers as whole systems, from the mountain streams that feed them to the deltas where they meet the sea.
